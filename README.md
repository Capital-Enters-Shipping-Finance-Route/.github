# How Capital Enters Shipping: A Map of Ship Finance Routes

## How Is Shipping Financed?

Shipping is financed through several routes, and bank lending is the largest. Petrofin Research estimated total global ship finance at about USD 680 billion at the end of 2025, of which bank loans accounted for about USD 425 billion, just over 60%. The remainder came from leasing, export finance, capital markets and alternative providers. The routes differ in who bears the risk, what supports the financing and which documents a capital provider reviews. For allocators and analysts, the route shapes the diligence.

## What Are the Main Ways Ship Finance Is Funded?

Five routes account for most activity. The features below are typical and vary by transaction.

| Route | Capital provider | What typically supports it | Main exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank loan | Commercial and regional banks | Vessel mortgage, charter earnings | Borrower credit, vessel value |
| Sale-and-leaseback | Leasing companies | Lessor's ownership of the vessel | Lessee credit, residual value |
| Bonds | Public debt markets | Issuer credit, sometimes security | Issuer credit, market access |
| Structured notes and ABS | Institutional buyers | Receivables held in an SPV | Receivables pool, structure |
| Funds and private credit | Specialist funds | Negotiated security packages | Higher leverage, bespoke terms |

**Institutional implication:** the route determines what is reviewed. A lender relies on the borrower and the mortgage, while a note holder relies on the receivables and servicer reporting.

## How Do Banks Fund Ships?

Petrofin's top 40 banks held USD 300.6 billion of shipping loans at the end of 2025, up 6% from USD 283.6 billion a year earlier. Petrofin's index of global ship finance, set at 100 in 2008, rose from 61 in 2024 to 63 in 2025, a level last seen in 2018. European banks held 50.4% of that total, and Japanese banks raised their share from 22% to 26%. In asset-backed ship lending, charter income is typically the main source of repayment and the vessel forms part of the security, as ARC Ratings describes in its methodology. Against a Clarksons-estimated fleet and orderbook value of USD 2.17 trillion, Petrofin concluded that the industry does not appear overleveraged.

**Institutional implication:** bank terms tend to depend on charter cover, the borrower's credit and the vessel's value.

## How Does Leasing Work?

In a sale-and-leaseback, the owner sells the vessel to a leasing company and leases it back, typically on a bareboat basis. In its report on 2024, Petrofin noted that Chinese, Japanese and Korean providers often offered loan-to-value ratios of 70–75% or more, which attracted owners seeking higher leverage. In 2025, US policy targeting Chinese vessels temporarily affected non-bank lending, and some owners refinanced Chinese leases into bank loans. Chinese leasing later resumed its growth.

**Institutional implication:** leasing moves the diligence toward the lessor's terms, purchase options and the ownership of the vessel during the lease.

## How Do Capital Markets Fund Shipping?

Shipping bonds raised USD 16.03 billion in 2025 (Clarksons, cited by Petrofin), while shipping IPOs and follow-on offerings were weaker. Securitization is used selectively. Container-lease ABS, backed by leases on containers rather than vessels, has issued rated notes since the 1990s. Issuance paused for roughly three years after 2008 and resumed in 2010 (Fitch). Vessel-based structures have appeared in individual transactions, such as the UASC maritime trust certificates, which were supported by bareboat charter receivables on ultra-large container vessels.

**Institutional implication:** note holders rely on the receivables, the structure and servicer reporting, not on direct ownership of a vessel.

## What Other Private Capital Participates?

Petrofin reported that specialist shipping funds offered tailor-made terms that attracted owners seeking higher leverage. Export finance sits within the total Petrofin measures, alongside leasing and alternative providers. Splash247's summary of the 2025 report described leasing, export credit, regional lenders, funds and private capital as taking a larger role than before the financial crisis.

**Institutional implication:** negotiated terms mean negotiated documentation, so analysts should expect bespoke covenants rather than standard packages.

## What Do Capital Providers Review Across Routes?

| Area | What is reviewed |
|---|---|
| Asset | Vessel type, age, flag state, classification status, statutory certificates, drydocking and survey history |
| Charter | Charter type and period, charterer, charter rate, termination terms |
| Valuation | Independent valuer, methodology, valuation date |
| Legal structure | Ownership, SPV documents, security, jurisdiction |
| Counterparty | Credit of the borrower, lessee or charterer |
| Reporting | Financial statements, technical manager reports, audit trail |

These headings apply to every route. The weighting differs: a bank loan emphasises the borrower and the mortgage, while structured notes emphasise the receivables and the servicer.

**Institutional implication:** the checklist describes what is reviewed. Participants assess suitability independently.

## Where Do Tokenized Structures Sit Among These Routes?

Tokenization is a way of representing and administering economic exposure. It does not replace the routes above. Shipfinex is distributing RWA Tokens known as Maritime Asset Tokens (MATs), which represent economic exposure to vessel-owning SPVs. The SPV holds the vessel, and the exposure relates to its charter earnings after operating costs. The review headings in the table above apply equally: vessel documentation, charter terms, independent valuation, legal structure and reporting. Details sit in the Data Room documentation and the Risk Disclosure Statement.

**Institutional implication:** tokenization changes how exposure is recorded and administered. It does not change the diligence the underlying vessel requires.

## What Are the Risks and Limitations?

Shipping is a cyclical industry. Charter rates, vessel values and financing terms have moved through cycles, and every route above is exposed to them. Contracted charter income does not remove vessel, counterparty, market, operational or structural risk. Secondary market liquidity may be limited or unavailable, particularly for structured and tokenized instruments. The figures here describe conditions to the end of 2025. Factors that have affected rates include trade volumes, fleet supply, orderbook deliveries and regulation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is ship finance?** Funding for buying, building or refinancing vessels, through loans, leases, bonds, structured notes or other instruments.

**Who provides it?** Banks, leasing companies, bond markets, specialist funds and export credit providers.

**Is leasing different from a loan?** Typically yes. In a lease the lessor owns the vessel, while in a loan the owner holds title and grants the lender a mortgage.

**What is container ABS?** Notes backed by lease receivables on containers rather than vessels.
